Papers I Love #2

By Tamara Paris

Discussed paper
The Forgotten Margins of AI Ethics June 2022
Abeba Birhane, Elayne Ruane, Thomas Laurent, Matthew S. Brown, Johnathan Flowers, Anthony Ventresque, and Christopher L. Dancy
ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAccT 2022)

📄 Read the paper

I read this paper for the first time a year and a half ago, right after I submitted my very first research paper to FAccT 2025. I remember reading it early in the morning, coffee in hand, sitting on the living room rug with my laptop on the coffee table. Usually, I only read for as long as it takes to finish my coffee, so I rarely finish the papers I start. This time, I read it all in one sitting.

I would describe this paper as a critical view on the AI ethics research, in particular on fairness literature. It outlines three main families that shape the field: philosophical work grounded in the Western practice of ethics, mathematical and statistical work, and critical work. Through an analysis of papers published at FAccT and AIES in preceding years, the article describes how these two communities remain heavily focused on producing the first two families of works, even though the last one represents a more effective path forward to genuinely help the communities most affected by power asymmetries in AI. The paper uses one quote from Heinz von Foerster and Bernhard Pörksen that I find summarizes perfectly their message:

"Ethics is a matter of practice, of down-to-earth problems and not a matter of those categories and taxonomies that serve to fascinate the academic clubs and their specialists."

The paper I had just submitted featured one of those taxonomies criticized by Foerster’s and Pörksen’s quote. Of course, I am still very proud of that paper today, but it certainly fits right into the paradigm of Westernized philosophy and a culture of abstraction, or, at the very least, it adopted all of its conventions. Up to that point, my entire background had been steeped in French mathematical culture, which taught me that theory was the holy grail of intelligence and the most impressive thing I could do. Inevitably, I wanted to be a theorist much more than a practitioner. This was not only a reflection of my French culture but also a fear of conducting research that is regarded as too “feminine” and, therefore, not being taken seriously as a computer science researcher.

Naturally, I felt called out by this work. But, in this case, being called out is a great thing.

After this, I kept the message of this paper in mind in all the papers I contributed to. The shift between my first and second paper illustrate this perfectly. I still remember going to see my supervisors after reading the paper and telling them: "I want to do more concrete research." Then, instead of continuing the theoretical framing of the concept of openness in AI that I did in my first paper, I turned toward studying how this concept is framed in the media for my second paper, naming the institutions involved in this framing and the interests they hold. Of course, I still have a tremendous amount to learn; I’m far from the kind of critical, deeply grounded research that some scholars produce. But it has definitely shifted my approach.

---

A few days ago, I realized that although this paper marked a shift in my own approach to research, I have never cited it in any of my subsequent work. It suffers a bit from the curse of contributions that are hard to characterize or to make visible: it didn't directly contribute to the advancement of my specific research topic, but rather to my development as a researcher, so I usually can’t find a place to cite it in my papers. It has implicitly shaped the angle of my research questions, my writing style, and my interpretation of results, but in a way that is hard to identify clearly, even by myself.

Today, my training as an AI ethics researcher has been supplemented by many other amazing papers along a similar line of work (many of which were written by Abeba Birhane!). This includes “The Values Encoded in Machine Learning Research” by Birhane et al. and “Rigor in AI: Doing Rigorous AI Work Requires a Broader, Responsible AI-Informed Conception of Rigor” by Olteanu et al., two works with similar flavor that I also highly recommend.
